General Zachary Taylor

Military Titan & Defender of the Union

"I have no private feelings to indulge, no personal preferences to gratify, no party obligations to perform. My guide shall be the Constitution."

Zachary Taylor was a career officer in the United States Army who rose to national fame as a major general during the Mexican-American War. Known affectionately by his troops as "Old Rough and Ready" for his unpretentious dress and steadfast demeanor, he became the first U.S. president elected without having previously held public office. Despite being a Southern slaveholder, Taylor strongly opposed the expansion of slavery into new Western territories and fiercely threatened military action against Southern secessionists to preserve the Union.

Military Heroics & Leadership

MAJOR GENERAL U.S. ARMY
1846

Battles of Palo Alto & Resaca de la Palma

Opening major engagements of the Mexican-American War. Taylor’s effective deployment of flying artillery defeated superior numerical forces and secured northern Rio Grande territory.

Rio Grande Border Region
1846

Battle of Monterrey

Taylor led a grueling urban assault against fortified Mexican positions. After heavy fighting, he negotiated an 8-week armistice, demonstrating strategic restraint that drew both praise and political scrutiny from Washington.

Monterrey, Mexico
1847

The Battle of Buena Vista

Taylor's crowning achievement. With only 4,700 men against General Santa Anna’s force of over 15,000, Taylor held the mountain pass, catapulting him to legendary national status and the Whig nomination.

Presidency & The Sectional Crisis (1849–1850)

Stance on Slavery Expansion & Secession

Though Taylor owned plantations in Louisiana and Mississippi, he surprised Southern leaders by taking a firm nationalist stance. He urged California and New Mexico to draft state constitutions and apply directly for statehood, bypassing territorial status where slavery could be debated.

When Southern leaders threatened secession over the admission of California as a free state, Taylor forcefully declared that he would personally lead the army to hang anyone caught in rebellion against the Union.

Clayton-Bulwer Treaty (1850)

On the foreign policy front, Secretary of State John M. Clayton negotiated an agreement with Great Britain ensuring that any future canal across Central America connecting the Atlantic and Pacific oceans would be neutral and unfortified.

Untimely Tragic End

Sudden Death in Office

On July 4, 1850, during fundraising ceremonies at the unfinished Washington Monument, President Taylor consumed raw cherries and iced milk on a scorching day. He fell severely ill with acute gastroenteritis (cholera morbus) and passed away five days later on July 9, 1850.

Succeeded by Vice President Millard Fillmore, who subsequently signed the Compromise of 1850 into law.
